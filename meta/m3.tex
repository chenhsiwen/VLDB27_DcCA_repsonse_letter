\paragraph{Meta-3 (Practical realization: constraints, validation, and system overheads).}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \textit{``Clarify the practical realization of the framework, including the cost of enforcing constraints, validating feasibility, and maintaining any constraint-aware indexing structures (construction, maintenance and updates).''}
\end{itemize}
\smallskip

\noindent\textbf{Response.}
\hw{The key intuition is that supporting constraints does \emph{not} require validating every instance in the database or rebuilding indexes for every query. We revised the paper to explain this more intuitively.

\textbf{Constraints are enforced coarse-to-fine.} The system first uses cheap constraints to quickly discard obviously infeasible candidates during retrieval, and applies expensive feasibility checks (e.g., temporal, graph, or semantic validation) only to the small set of promising candidates. Expensive validation therefore runs only when necessary, and its cost depends on the candidate set, not the whole database.

\textbf{Indexes are built once and reused.} Instead of one index per specification $\omega$, the system groups similar specifications into a few structural families that share the same index. Index construction is thus amortized across queries, and database updates are handled incrementally. If a query's $\omega$ falls outside the predefined families, the system simply falls back to a linear scan, so the framework stays correct while gracefully trading efficiency for flexibility.

\textbf{Overall overhead is dominated by offline indexing, paid once.} Online processing is lightweight: candidate generation does cheap pruning, expensive validation is confined to a small pool, and set-level optimization operates only on that pool. Consequently, once candidate retrieval completes, online cost is largely independent of database size---confirmed by our scalability study (Table~\ref{tab:scale}), where the set-selection step is essentially constant in $N$. For space, we report these index-construction and maintenance costs in a ``Practical realization and costs'' paragraph in Appendix~A, while constraint enforcement and feasibility validation are detailed in Section~2.2.}

\noindent\emph{Revision (feasibility validation in Section~3):}
\begin{quote}
\textcolor{blue}{The coarse-to-fine design bounds the cost of constraint enforcement and feasibility validation. Cheap attribute- and metric-level checks are performed during candidate generation (Step~2.a), pruning most infeasible instances before expensive validation. Execution-level (temporal, graph) and semantic (text, image) validation is then applied only to the candidate pool $\mathcal A_\omega(q)$, so validation scales with $|\mathcal A_\omega|$ rather than $|\mathcal D|$. Because candidates are processed in nondecreasing cost order, validation terminates once $k$ admissible answers are found. Increasing $m$ improves the likelihood of finding $k$ feasible counterfactuals at the cost of additional validation, providing a controllable accuracy--latency trade-off (Principle~P3 in Section~3.2).}
\end{quote}

\noindent\emph{Revision (Appendix~A, ``Practical realization and costs''):}
\begin{quote}
\textcolor{blue}{The offline index is amortized across queries: the specification family $\mathcal W$ is partitioned into a small number of structural families (metric-reweighting vs.\ discrete-constraint dominated), each served by one reusable index, so construction cost scales with the number of families rather than $|\mathcal W|$. Under insertions and deletions to $\mathcal D$ the index is maintained incrementally, and a query whose $\omega\notin\mathcal W$ falls back to a linear scan, so the cost model degrades gracefully.}
\end{quote}
